Free Hubstaff alternative: what to look for

A buyer's checklist for replacing Hubstaff without a per-seat bill: the seven things a free time tracker has to get right, and where most of them quietly fall short.

October 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Most teams start looking for a free Hubstaff alternative for one of two reasons: the per-seat bill stopped making sense, or someone asked an uncomfortable question about what the agent actually records. Both are good reasons. Neither is a good reason to grab the first "free forever" tracker on a listicle.

Free trackers fail in predictable places. Below is the checklist we use ourselves — seven questions, in the order they usually bite — plus how to answer each one in under ten minutes.

1. Is it free, or free until you are dependent on it?

"Free" covers at least four different business models:

  • Free tier with a seat cap. Fine at five people, a wall at six.
  • Free trial dressed up as a plan. The clock is in the fine print.
  • Free client, paid server. The agent costs nothing; storing what it collects does.
  • Open source. Free because you can run it yourself, forever.

Only the last one is structurally free. Everything else is a price you have not been quoted yet. Before you roll anything out, find the page that says what happens at seat number 11, and what happens to data you already collected if you stop paying.

2. Where does the data actually live?

This is the question that decides whether a migration is possible later. A cloud-only tracker owns your history. If the vendor raises prices, gets acquired, or has an outage during payroll week, you have no local copy to fall back on.

Ask for three specifics:

  • Is there a local store on the machine, and does tracking keep working offline?
  • What is the export format — CSV and an API, or a PDF report?
  • Can you point the agent at your own backend?

A tracker that is local-first and self-hostable can never strand you. One that is neither is a subscription you cannot leave without losing the record.

3. What does it record, exactly?

There is a real line between measuring work and reading over someone's shoulder, and plenty of monitoring tools quietly cross it. The ones that matter:

  • Keyboard — reasonable: keystroke counts per interval. Crossing the line: logging what was actually typed.
  • Screen — reasonable: periodic screenshots with a retention limit. Crossing the line: continuous screen recording.
  • Browser — reasonable: page title and time on page. Crossing the line: form field contents and saved credentials.
  • Apps — reasonable: app and window time, idle-aware. Crossing the line: reading document contents.

If a vendor cannot tell you which side of each line it sits on, assume the wrong one. For free tools specifically, also ask what is done with the data in aggregate — if the product has no revenue model you can see, the data is the revenue model.

4. Is idle time handled honestly?

A tracker that counts a machine sitting on a login screen as eight hours of work is worse than no tracker, because now you have numbers and they are wrong. Look for:

  • An explicit idle threshold, and idle gaps that are excluded from active time rather than silently filled in.
  • Visible idle periods in the timeline, so a reviewer can see the shape of the day.
  • No "keep me active" workaround that makes the number meaningless anyway.

Test it: install the agent, go make coffee for fifteen minutes, and check whether the day total moved.

5. Does it run properly on the machines you actually have?

Free trackers are frequently one real app plus a thin port. On macOS, screen recording, accessibility and input monitoring each need a separate permission grant, and an app that handles those badly either fails silently or nags forever. On Windows, an unsigned installer means a SmartScreen warning every single employee will have to click past.

Install it on one machine of each OS you own before you decide anything. Fifteen minutes here saves a rollout.

6. Can the person being tracked see what you see?

Monitoring that people discover rather than agree to is the fastest way to turn a tooling decision into a trust problem. The practical test is whether the employee can open their own data: today's timeline, the screenshots that were taken, the signals being collected.

A tool built with that in mind will have it as a visible screen. A tool built around surveillance will have it as a support ticket.

7. Is there a dashboard, or just a database?

Self-hosted and open-source options sometimes win on every point above and then lose on this one: the data is all there, and reading it means SQL. If you are replacing Hubstaff, you are also replacing its reports. Check that someone who is not you can answer "what did this team do last week" without help.

The short version

Score any candidate out of seven: structurally free, local-first storage, documented and proportionate signals, honest idle handling, real support for your OS mix, transparency to the person tracked, and a usable dashboard. Most free trackers score three or four. That is fine — as long as you know which three before you roll it out, not after.

How BiBoTracking answers these

We built BiBoTracking because we kept scoring badly on points 1, 2 and 6. It is open source and free for unlimited employees, stores activity locally first and syncs to a backend you can host yourself, records keystroke counts rather than keystrokes, hatches out idle gaps instead of counting them, keeps screenshots on a retention limit, and gives owners a web dashboard rather than a database connection string. On point 5 we are honest about where we score badly: the macOS app is signed and notarised, the Windows installer is not yet, so Windows users still see a SmartScreen warning on first run.
