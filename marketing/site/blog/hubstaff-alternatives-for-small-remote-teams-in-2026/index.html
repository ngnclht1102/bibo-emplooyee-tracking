Hubstaff alternatives for small remote teams in 2026

An honest Hubstaff alternative guide for 3-15 person remote teams: real pricing, what you actually use, and when switching is a waste of time.

October 27, 2026 · 6 min read

I run a small software team and I have paid for Hubstaff. Most people searching for a Hubstaff alternative are not angry at the product — it works. They are annoyed that a 6-person team is paying for a platform built for 200 people, and that nobody on the team uses more than a fifth of it. This post is the rundown I wish I had read before I started comparing tabs.

What Hubstaff actually costs a small team

The number that surprises owners is not the per-seat price, it is the seat minimum and the plan jump.

Hubstaff is priced per seat per month, with a two-seat minimum, and it ships in tiers. The cheap tier gives you time tracking and basic activity. Screenshots, app and URL tracking, and most of the reporting that owners actually open live one or two tiers up. So the real math for a team of eight is rarely "eight times the headline price" — it is eight times the tier that contains the one feature you came for.

Two more things that inflate the bill:

  • Annual vs monthly. The advertised price is usually the annual one. Monthly billing costs more, and monthly is what most small teams want while they are still figuring out if the tool sticks.
  • Add-ons. Scheduling, payroll integrations, and the extras are often priced separately from the base seat. Each one is small. Together they are not.

Prices change, so check the current page before you quote me. The structure is the part worth understanding: you pay for a tier, not for features, and small teams land in a tier where they use maybe three of fifteen things.

The complexity cost nobody puts in a spreadsheet

The money is the easy part. The thing that actually pushed me to look around was setup time.

Hubstaff is a platform: projects, tasks, clients, budgets, schedules, pay rates, invoicing, work orders, achievement badges, dozens of report types. If you are an agency billing clients by the hour, that depth is the reason you bought it. If you are five developers and a designer, you spend an afternoon configuring an organisation chart you do not have, and then you ignore 80% of the dashboard forever.

There is also a real human cost. The more surveillance surface a tool exposes — keystroke counts, idle shaming, activity percentage scores — the more your team reads it as distrust. A big platform gives you every dial. You then have to decide, in public, which dials you are choosing not to turn.

How to pick a Hubstaff alternative without wasting a month

Do this before you open a single pricing page. It takes twenty minutes and it kills most of your shortlist.

1. Write down the one question you need answered. Not "monitor the team." The actual question. Mine was: "did the contractor I pay hourly work the hours on the invoice, and roughly on what?" Someone else's is "are we losing two hours a day to context switching?" These two questions have completely different answers in tooling.

2. Decide if you need screenshots, honestly. Screenshots are the dividing line in this market. If your answer is "it would be nice to have," you do not need them, and you can use something simpler and cheaper. If you are paying hourly contractors you have never met in person, you probably do, and you should tell them so in writing before day one.

3. Count your real seat number for the next 12 months. Not your hiring plan. Your actual team. A tool that is cheap at 8 seats and painful at 40 is fine if you will not see 40 seats.

4. Check the desktop app on the machines you own. This is where tools quietly fail. Ask: does it run native on Apple Silicon, does it survive sleep and wake, does it keep tracking offline and sync later, does macOS screen-recording permission need to be re-granted after every update. A tracker that loses an afternoon of offline work is worse than a spreadsheet.

5. Run a two-week trial with two people, not everyone. Pick yourself and one person who will tell you the truth. Then export the data and see if it answers the question from step 1. If the export is a mess, the tool is a mess.

The shortlist, and who each one is actually for

  • Time Doctor — the closest like-for-like. Same screenshot-and-activity model, strong reporting, similar pricing posture. Switch here if you like Hubstaff's approach but dislike Hubstaff specifically.
  • Toggl Track — the best choice if you realise you wanted timesheets, not monitoring. Lovely to use, genuinely liked by employees, no screenshots. Owners who want proof of work will be disappointed; that is by design.
  • Clockify — the budget answer. A real free tier that holds up for small teams, paid plans that undercut almost everyone. Shallower on the monitoring side, and the free tier is where most small teams actually live. I went deeper on the free-tier tradeoffs in what to look for in a free Hubstaff alternative.
  • DeskTime — productivity-scoring focus. Useful if your question is about focus and distraction rather than billable hours.
  • ActivTrak — analytics for workforce behaviour, built for larger orgs and compliance-minded buyers. Overkill at 8 seats, and priced like it.

The honest summary: Toggl and Clockify if you want hours, Time Doctor and the screenshot tools if you want evidence, ActivTrak if you want behavioural analytics and have a budget.

The migration part people skip

Two practical warnings from doing this badly once.

Your history does not move. Most tools will not import another tool's time entries in any usable way. Export everything to CSV before you cancel, and keep it somewhere you will find it in two years when someone disputes an invoice. Switch at a month or quarter boundary so your records have a clean seam.

Tell the team before the new agent installs itself. A new monitoring app appearing on someone's laptop unannounced costs you more trust than the tool will ever earn back. One short message: what is recorded, what is not, who can see it, and why. If you cannot write that message comfortably, you have picked the wrong settings.

Also check your legal position. In the EU and UK, monitoring employees needs a lawful basis and a proportionality argument, and some US states require notice. This is boring and it is cheaper than the alternative.

Where we fit, and where we do not

I built bibotracker because of the gap in step 1: a desktop app for macOS and Windows that records work time, takes periodic screenshots and browser activity, and a web admin where an owner can see who worked on what. That is the whole product. It is aimed at owners of 3-15 person remote teams who want that question answered and nothing else configured.

It is the wrong choice if you bill clients hourly and need invoicing, if you need payroll runs and scheduling in the same tool, if you want deep productivity analytics, or if you have 100 seats and a compliance team — those are Hubstaff, Time Doctor and ActivTrak problems, and they solve them better than we will.
