Time Doctor vs BiBoTracking: what you actually pay for

An honest founder's look at Time Doctor pricing, the add-ons and seats that drive the bill, and how to pick a time doctor alternative for a small team.

October 27, 2026 · 6 min read

I build BiBoTracking, so treat this as a biased source. I'm still going to try to be useful, because most "Time Doctor alternative" posts are written by people who have never paid either bill. I have. The thing nobody explains clearly is that the sticker price on a per-user plan is maybe 60% of what you end up spending, and the other 40% is the part that actually decides whether the tool sticks.

Time Doctor is a real product with a long track record. It's been around since 2012, it's built for teams that need proof of work, and it is the default pick for a lot of agencies and BPOs. If you have 40 people across three countries and a client who wants screenshots attached to invoices, Time Doctor will do that. I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

What I want to break down is the bill.

The four things you actually pay for

Pricing pages change, so check the current numbers yourself before you quote me. The structure, though, has been stable for years, and the structure is what matters.

Seats. Time Doctor is priced per user per month, with a cheaper rate if you pay annually. Run the math on your real headcount, not your current one. At a mid-tier plan of roughly ten dollars per user per month, a six-person team is around $720 a year. A twelve-person team is around $1,440. That's the easy part, and it's the part everyone compares.

Tiers. This is where the real money is. The entry plan gives you time tracking and basic activity. The things small teams actually ask for after month two — payroll, integrations, the better reports, client login access — tend to sit one or two tiers up. So you don't pay the entry price. You pay the entry price for two months, hit a wall, and then pay roughly double. Budget for the tier you'll be on in six months.

Minimums and billing shape. Check whether there's a seat minimum and what happens when someone leaves. Monthly billing is more expensive per seat but you can drop seats quickly. Annual billing is cheaper and locks you in. For a team that fluctuates — contractors, seasonal help — the "cheaper" annual plan is often the more expensive choice, because you pay for empty seats for nine months.

Your own time. The cost nobody puts in the spreadsheet. Onboarding eleven people onto a tool with a large settings surface is a real afternoon, sometimes two. Then there's the ongoing part: someone has to answer "why did it log me as idle", fix the one Windows laptop where the agent won't start, and decide what the productivity ratings mean. On a small team, that someone is you. If a tool costs you two hours a month, that's more than the subscription.

Picking a time doctor alternative: ask these four questions first

The mistake I made when I was shopping was comparing feature lists. Feature lists are useless because every tool in this space has thirty features and you'll use five. Compare answers to these instead.

Do you need surveillance or do you need a timesheet?

These are different products sold under the same banner. A timesheet answers "how many hours, on what". Monitoring answers "was this person working". If all you need is hours for invoicing, you don't need screenshots, and Toggl Track or Clockify will cost you less and annoy your team less. Be honest about which problem you have. I wrote more about separating those two needs in Hubstaff alternatives for small remote teams in 2026.

Who is going to look at the data?

If the answer is "nobody, most weeks", buy the cheapest thing that records hours. Productivity scores, work-life balance dashboards and activity heatmaps are only worth money if a human reviews them and changes something. Otherwise you're paying for a report nobody opens.

How many of the modules will you switch on?

Count them. Payroll, invoicing, client logins, project budgets, integrations with your PM tool, idle detection, scheduling, attendance. On a six-person team my honest guess is you'll use three. You're paying for eight.

Will your team accept it?

This is the one that kills deployments. A tool your team quietly resents produces bad data: people leave the timer running, or they stop the tracker during real work because it feels invasive. Whatever you pick, tell people exactly what's recorded and what isn't, before you install anything. "There are periodic screenshots and we keep them for 30 days" lands much better than someone discovering it later.

Where BiBoTracking is different, and where it isn't

BiBoTracking is narrower on purpose. A desktop app for macOS and Windows records work time, takes periodic screenshots and logs browser activity. A web admin shows the owner who worked on what. That's the whole product. No payroll, no invoicing, no client portals, no productivity score that grades your team out of 100.

The reason is the third question above. I built it for the case where someone needs to see that work happened, with evidence, and doesn't want to administer a platform to get there. Setup is installing the app and signing in. There's no plan tier that unlocks the feature you wanted.

The tradeoff is real, so here it is. If you need hours to flow into payroll automatically, BiBoTracking won't do that — you'll export and handle it elsewhere. If you need per-client invoicing, it won't do that either. If you want your client to log in and see their own project's activity, Time Doctor has that and I don't.

Where BiBoTracking is the wrong choice

Three cases, plainly.

You're above roughly 30 people. At that size you need role hierarchies, delegated admins and bulk management. Time Doctor and ActivTrak are built for that. I'm not.

You're a freelancer or a two-person shop billing by the hour. Screenshot monitoring is overkill and a free Toggl or Clockify account will serve you better. If free is where you're starting, I went through what actually matters in a free tier in Free Hubstaff alternative: what to look for.

Your team works on Linux, on mobile, or mostly outside a computer. The tracking lives in a desktop app. No desktop, no data.

How to decide without wasting a month

Pick two tools. Run each on your own machine plus two volunteers for one full week — not a day, a week, including a Monday and a Friday. Then check three things: did the hours match what those three people say they worked, how many support questions did you field, and how long did you actually spend in the admin screen. The tool that wins on those three is the one to buy, regardless of which has the longer feature list.

If what you need is the narrow version — record time, capture screenshots and browser activity, see it in one admin screen, nothing else to configure — that's what bibotracker does, and you can try it on your own laptop before you involve anyone else.
